Fleet benchmark

A single service carries only one or two labelled incident windows in its hold-out, which is too thin to rank detectors on. This runs the identical pipeline across every labelled service and aggregates two ways — a macro average that weights each service equally, and pooled counts that weight each observation equally. Where the two disagree, no model dominates, and the summary says so.

The problem

Cloud services emit a continuous stream of telemetry — CPU, memory, network, request counts, disk throughput. Incidents announce themselves in that stream before customers notice, but almost always at a scale and shape that fixed thresholds miss.

The failure mode is specific and expensive. A static ceiling calibrated for peak hours is blind to an outage at 04:00 when load legitimately collapses; one calibrated for the trough pages every single weekday morning. Teams respond by widening thresholds until the alerts stop, at which point the detector has an effective recall of zero.

Intended users: site-reliability and platform engineers who own service health, and the on-call rota that has to act on whatever the system decides to escalate.

The approach

Model what the metric should be, then alert on the gap — so the threshold moves with time of day instead of ignoring it.

The hybrid runs in two stages. SARIMAX with Fourier seasonal regressors captures the linear autocorrelation and the daily cycle. A nonlinear learner is then trained on what that model leaves in its residuals, following Zhang's hybrid decomposition of a series into linear and nonlinear parts. Detection fuses a train-calibrated robust residual z-score with an Isolation Forest over residual shape, then debounces to suppress single-bucket noise.

Six approaches are compared on identical splits, including two deliberately simple baselines. If the complexity does not beat them, the leaderboard says so.

Evaluation stance

Forecast accuracy is reported as MAE, RMSE, MAPE, sMAPE and MASE. MASE matters here because byte and request counts legitimately idle near zero, where MAPE is undefined or explodes.

Detection is scored point-wise and point-adjusted. Reporting only point-wise F1 flatters detectors that fire constantly; reporting only point-adjusted F1 hides alert fatigue. Both appear, alongside detection latency and false alarms per day — the two numbers that actually decide whether a team leaves an alert switched on.

The composite ranking weights point-adjusted F1 at 0.45, segment recall 0.25, point-wise F1 0.15, latency 0.10 and alert burden 0.05.

Data and honesty

The bundled corpus is the realAWSCloudwatch family of the Numenta Anomaly Benchmark: genuine Amazon CloudWatch server telemetry at five-minute resolution over roughly two weeks per series, with human-curated anomaly windows rather than point labels.

Window labels are generous by design — they open before the visible excursion so that early detection is rewarded. That inflates measured detection latency relative to what an operator would perceive, and the figures here are not adjusted to hide it.

Splits are strictly chronological. Thresholds and residual scales are calibrated on training data only, never on the window being judged.
